Executive Summary · 48 findings · 20 Jul 2026

A World-Title Property, Quietly Ceding Rate, Recency, and the Rooftop on surfaces it doesn’t control

The Dewberry’s fundamentals are the envy of its market: Virtuoso’s 2025 Best Independent Hotel in the World, a MICHELIN Key, 68,000 Instagram followers, guests who praise staff by name, and same-day scores of 9.6 on Expedia, 9.5 on US News, and 4.6 on Google. This audit found the equity intact — and leaking through three seams. First, the money seam: the property’s own Book Now buttons ship with stale, hard-coded analytics identities that corrupt attribution at the exact website-to-booking-engine handoff where it matters (M-01), while on Google’s booking panel a wholesale reseller undercuts the official rate by 12.5% and on KAYAK the hotel isn’t listed as a seller at all (D-01, D-02). Second, the truth seam: the two largest OTA families are still selling Henrietta’s — a restaurant closed since 2020 — as a live four-meal amenity, and the fiction traces upstream to the hotel’s own dining-page metadata (D-05, M-05); the schema entity card Google reads declares a $180–$300 price range against a live $419–$779 rate card (M-02, D-04). Third, the reputation seam: Tripadvisor’s recency-weighted display has slid to 4.0 and #26 in the city while every other platform holds the property in the top band — and the arithmetic in Chapter 08 proves the slide is a weighting artifact riding on ~2.7 reviews a month of unmanaged volume, which means the same math will reward a managed stream just as fast (R-02, B-01, R-05). None of the three seams requires a rebrand, a renovation, or a rebuild. They require ownership of the surfaces — a twelve-week remediation arc (Ch.10), an agent layer that keeps it true (Ch.09), and a right-sized engagement to run it (Ch.11).

F-02Moderate
Where H1s do exist, a template pattern strips their heading semantics with role="region"

Two Platforms, Two Deep Reads: the outlier and the anchor

Chapter 07 established the divergence; this chapter dissects the two platforms that matter most to it. Part A takes apart Tripadvisor — the outlier — and shows arithmetically that the 4.0 headline cannot be the corpus average and must be recency weighting broadcasting a small weak cohort. Part B reads Google — the anchor — whose 4.6 holds, but whose own highlighting choices and syndication variants carry the complaint themes into places the property doesn’t watch.

Methodology — what this sample is, and is not
Record-level review scraping is bot-gated across the industry, and this audit does not pretend otherwise. Tripadvisor’s record pages returned 403 via DataDome (verbatim interstitial: “Please enable JS and disable any ad blocker”). Booking.com’s review endpoints are robots-disallowed and returned a 202 bot-challenge; its listed corpus at the gate was n=90, too thin and too gated to headline, so Part A pivots to Tripadvisor — the platform actually broadcasting the problem. The working sample is therefore: platform-published aggregates and distributions, machine summaries, and individually indexed review exemplars (title + body fragments with dates), all captured 20 Jul 2026. Conclusions below are drawn only where that sample supports them; everything else is labeled a bound, not a fact.

Part A · Family BTripadvisor — anatomy of a 4.0 that isn’t an average

How the platform’s number works, in one paragraph. Tripadvisor documents that its bubble rating and Popularity Index are not simple means: they weight by recency (new reviews count more, and old ones decay), quantity (thin recent volume makes the display volatile), and quality of contribution. In practice a property with a large, aging, excellent corpus and a trickle of mixed recent reviews can display a number far below its true average — and a property that manages fresh volume can move its display far faster than the corpus math would suggest. Both edges of that blade are visible in the findings below.

B-01Moderate
The arithmetic: even 79 straight one-bubble reviews couldn’t drag a true average from 4.6 to 4.0 — the headline is recency weighting, and it will respond to recency
Run the worst case. At the n=1,437 snapshot the display was 4.6. Assume every one of the 79 reviews added since then was a 1.0 — an impossibly bad streak. The true mean would be (1,437 × 4.6 + 79 × 1.0) ÷ 1,516 = 4.41, which Tripadvisor rounds to 4.5. The displayed 4.0 is therefore not the corpus average; it is Tripadvisor’s recency-and-quality weighting amplifying a small, recent, weak cohort. That cuts both ways: the same math that produced the slide will reward a managed stream of fresh positives just as disproportionately. This is the single most actionable number in the reputation stack.
